An exchange is the sharpest possible exercise in concurrency. Orders arrive from everywhere, every one of them expects atomicity, and the difference between a matching engine that works and one that works *correctly* is invisible — until it isn't. This project is a miniature exchange built to understand exactly how far relational databases and good locking discipline can go before you need to invent your own data structure.

## Problem

Why does this exist?

Order books are the heart of every market. The naive version is trivial: keep a list, match the best price, done. The real version has to answer hard questions under load:

- What happens when two orders arrive at the same millisecond for the same price level?
- How do you keep the ledger consistent when a single trade touches users, portfolios, and audit logs?
- How do you serve live order book data at low latency while still persisting every event durably?
- Where does the line between the relational store and the fast path actually fall?

## The core concepts

### ACID

Every trade is a single transaction. Either the order, the trade, both portfolios, and the audit rows all commit together, or none of them do. There is no partial state — an exchange that half-writes a trade is worse than an exchange that rejects it. Trade execution is ACID-compliant end to end, with transactional rollback as the safety net.

### Isolation

Concurrent trades on the same instrument must not observe each other mid-flight. I worked through the four isolation levels against a concrete scenario — two orders, one book, zero lost updates — and the answer was a mix of `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE` on the affected rows and optimistic locking via version columns.

### Locks

The interesting decision is *what* you lock. Lock the whole order book and you serialize everything. Lock a single price level and you keep parallelism between different levels while still protecting the level that is being contested. I lock the specific instrument/price rows the trade touches — row-level locking without ever locking the full book.

### Matching Engine

Price-time priority is the rule: best price first, and for equal prices, earlier orders first. Market orders hit the best available price immediately; limit orders rest on the book until they match.

### Order Book

The book itself is a two-sided structure — bids and asks — kept hot in Redis. Reads never touch Postgres; the database owns the durable truth and the ledger, while Redis owns the millisecond view.

## What I actually learned

### Relational tools go further than expected

`SELECT FOR UPDATE`, optimistic locking, materialized views, window functions, triggers, indexing, and `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` turned PostgreSQL into a real exchange backend. The row-locking strategy handled the concurrency scenarios I threw at it — including a deliberately engineered deadlock, which I then had to prevent.

### The fast path and the durable path are separate jobs

Redis serves the hot order book; Postgres is the ledger of record; Kafka carries trades to analytics and notifications. The matching engine is the only component that has to be both correct and synchronous.

### Deadlocks are a design error, not a runtime surprise

Once I reproduced a deadlock by issuing two overlapping transactions in different orders, the fix was obvious: lock rows in a consistent global order. `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` showed the index that made the second transaction stop blocking on the first.

## Benchmarks

| Scenario | Mechanism | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concurrent market orders, same level | Row locks on instrument/price | No lost trades, no serialized full book |
| Overlapping updates to one portfolio | Optimistic locking (version column) | Retries resolve conflicts, no overwrites |
| Order book reads | Redis cache | Millisecond reads, DB untouched |
| Async trade fan-out | Kafka → analytics/notifications | Decoupled from the matching critical path |

## Lessons Learned

- **Isolation is a product decision** — pick the weakest level that provably preserves your invariants.
- **Locks are a granularity decision** — too coarse serializes you, too fine deadlocks you.
- **The ledger is sacred** — every mutating path goes through a transaction; there is no out-of-band write.
- **Measure with `EXPLAIN ANALYZE`** — indexes are where your "scale problems" live.
