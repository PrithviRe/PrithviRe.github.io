Ray marching is the inverse of polygon rendering — instead of projecting triangles onto a screen, you march rays from the camera into a scene defined entirely by mathematical functions. No meshes, no vertices, no triangles. Every surface exists as an equation.

## Problem

Traditional rasterization needs geometry. Ray marching needs only a function that answers "how far is the nearest surface from this point?" — a Signed Distance Function (SDF). The challenge is making that evaluation fast enough to run per-pixel, every frame, with lighting, shadows, and ambient occlusion.

## Signed distance functions

The scene is defined by composing primitive SDFs — spheres, boxes, planes, tori — using CSG operators:

- **Union** — combine two shapes.
- **Subtraction** — carve one shape out of another.
- **Intersection** — keep only the overlap.
- **Smooth minimum (`smin`)** — blend shapes organically, producing metaball-like morphing.

## Lighting

The lighting engine goes beyond basic diffuse shading:

- **Blinn-Phong specular** — sharp highlights from a half-vector calculation.
- **Numerical normals** — surface normals computed via tetrahedron gradient sampling of the SDF, no analytical derivatives needed.
- **Soft penumbra shadows** — ray-marched shadows with a penumbra factor that softens edges based on closest-pass distance.
- **Multi-tap ambient occlusion** — samples along the normal direction estimate how much surrounding geometry occludes each point.

## 6-DOF camera

A fly camera with WASD keyboard integration and mouse look controls provides full six degrees of freedom across three procedurally rendered scenes.

## Lessons Learned

- **SDFs are composable** — complex geometry emerges from simple function composition, not complex mesh editing.
- **The GPU is a massively parallel ray marcher** — a fullscreen fragment shader evaluates millions of rays simultaneously.
- **Soft shadows are cheap** — the penumbra trick adds almost no cost to the shadow march but dramatically improves visual quality.
